## Core Positioning

Not "generic KDP books" and not only "Indian heritage for diaspora."

The shelf is:

> Yoga-rooted guides using mythology, practice, stillness, and art.

Indian mythology is a deep root and moat, but the broader line can include yoga, contemplative practice, deity guides, journals, spiritual art, family-safe mythology, and carefully separated adult visionary art.

## Voice

Warm, practitioner-led, specific, gentle, grounded, and non-dogmatic.

The books should sound like a real yogi/parent/teacher made them with care, not like an AI content farm. Use short readable sentences for kids, concrete sensory images, and plain emotional truth.

**Kids line** combines this brand voice with **dialogic-reading principles** (see Kids Line section below). Contemplative phrasings sit on parent-facing pages (grown-ups note, closing blessings, back-matter); the story pages are interactive.

## Use

- "In one well-known telling..."
- "Many families tell the story this way..."
- "A traditional meaning..."
- "This can remind us..."
- "In yoga, this can be understood as..."
- "This guide uses this as a contemplative correspondence, not the only valid lineage."

## Avoid

- fake scriptural certainty
- fake Sanskrit or pseudo-script
- fabricated quotes
- generic "ancient wisdom" haze
- generic self-care filler
- "hand drawn" if AI-generated
- medical or therapeutic claims
- violent details in kids books
- stoner/adult visionary branding near kids books
- "Bogus Yogi" or joke branding for kids religious/cultural books

## Kids Line

Series frame: `Little Guides to the Gods: Stories, Symbols & Coloring Pages`

Audience: **ages 3-7 primary** (read-aloud sweet spot, extending into early independent reading), parents/yoga families/diaspora families/homeschoolers/mythology-curious families.

### Voice — dialogic read-aloud, not contemplative literary (locked 2026-05-15)

The voice that works for this audience is **the parent reading aloud to a 3-7 year old**, not a contemplative adult reading to themselves. The story has to land twice in every paragraph: once for the kid, once for the parent doing the voices. Get this wrong and the book reads "beautifully written" to an adult but loses the child in 30 seconds.

This is **dialogic reading** — established early-literacy practice (Whitehurst & Lonigan; Trelease's *Read-Aloud Handbook*; the PEER framework — prompt, evaluate, expand, repeat). For this brand the techniques combine with A Modern Yogi's warmth/gentleness — *not* a generic hyper kids-book voice.

Techniques the kids line MUST use on story pages:

- **Interactive prompts that pull the child in.** "Do you like music? Krishna does!" beats "Krishna had a flute." Open with a question or a comparison the kid recognizes from their own life.
- **Repetition for emphasis and rhythm.** "Soft butter. Sweet butter. The BEST butter." Three-beat repetition builds rhythm; the third beat carries the punchline.
- **Dramatic emphasis** — caps, italics, or stretched words ("REALLY", "BEST", "HIGH") — used sparingly to mark the moments the parent's voice should pop. The parent reads it; the kid feels it.
- **Call-and-response and asides.** "Or so she thought." "Or did he?" Short pivots after a setup line are the joke beat that lands for the kid *and* the parent.
- **Shared laughter beats.** The funniest line should make the parent smirk too — that's the moment the kid sees the parent enjoying it, and the book becomes a shared thing. Emoji are okay *sparingly* as warmth/laughter markers in this read-aloud context (😄, 🧈) — they signal "this is a moment to enjoy together," not literary text.
- **Short impact lines.** Fragments. Sentences a 4-year-old can hold in their head. "Maakhan everywhere!" — done.
- **Hindi vernacular where culturally apt.** *Maakhan Chor*, *Mama Yashoda*, *namaste* — anchor the language; don't translate everything away. The first use can self-define in context; subsequent uses become shared touchstones. Never invent pseudo-Sanskrit or "ancient-sounding" words.
- **Onomatopoeia and sensory words** — sounds, textures, tastes — the kid hears the story in their body.

### Tone

- **story-first**, not lecture-first
- **interactive** — the kid is *in* the story, not *at* it
- **devotional without pressure**, **honest that traditions vary**
- **shared laughter** — funny moments land for both parent and child
- no gore, nudity, weapon glamour, or scary demon violence
- symbols become values children can carry, never enforced
- activities should feel useful, not filler — reflection prompts, simple breathing, draw-your-own pages

### Avoid (kids line specifically)

- **Literary register that loses the child.** *"He is more than a boy in a story — he is a small piece of the big love that holds the whole world together."* Beautiful to an adult; opaque to a 4-year-old. Contemplative phrasings of this type belong on parent-facing pages (notes for grown-ups, closing blessings) — never on story pages.
- **Lecturing the lesson.** "Krishna teaches us that kindness is brave." — too explicit. Show the kindness in a moment; let the parent and child notice it together.
- **Made-up Hindi or pseudo-Sanskrit.** Real terms only, anchored in context.
- **Heavy adjective stacking** that adult prose loves and children's brains can't track. Verbs and nouns carry kid prose; adjectives sit out the dance.
- **Translating every cultural term out of fear.** *Maakhan* doesn't need to become "butter" every time — the parent explains it the first time; the word becomes a shared touchstone.

### Use — kids line example phrasings (dialogic register)

- "Do you like X? Krishna does!"
- "Soft X. Sweet X. The BEST X."
- "She put it up HIGH. So high, little Krishna couldn't reach it. Or so she thought."
- "And right at the very top — Krishna!"
- "Maakhan everywhere! On his hands. On his nose. On his cheeks."
- "How could she be angry at her little Maakhan Chor?"

For parent-facing pages (grown-ups note, closing blessing, back-matter), the existing contemplative phrasings in the Use section at the top of this file still apply — "in one well-known telling," "many families tell the story this way."

### Structure — coloring book FIRST, story book second (locked 2026-05-16)

This series is sold and shelved as **kids coloring books**. The story is the soul, but the coloring page is the surface. A child opens the book and sees pictures. A parent picks it up and sees pictures. Amazon shoppers see the back-cover thumbnails and decide on pictures. Get the pictures wrong, the book fails — no matter how good the words are.

Hard rules for any new title in the line:

- **Every story page MUST be followed (or preceded) by a coloring page that depicts that story's content.** Story page on page N; coloring page that visualizes that story on page N±1. Lockstep. The child finishes the story page and immediately has something concrete to color from it.
- **Every coloring page MUST be a distinct character-driven scene from the book's story canon.** No two coloring pages share the same subject or composition. If two scenes feel similar, drop one.
- **Bring real mythological characters in, not just the title deity.** A book about Ganesha needs Parvati, Shiva, Kartikeya, Vyasa, Mooshik, and other named figures from the canon. A book about Hanuman needs Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, Jambavan. A book about Lakshmi/Saraswati needs Brahma, the hamsa swan named, the owl Uluka, the family in the humble-home folk story. Title-deity-alone-teaching produces repetitive imagery that reads "lame" and gives the child nothing varied to color.
- **No activity pages.** No match-the-symbols. No count-the-objects. No "draw your own X" framed activities. No symbol mandalas. No symbol icon sheets. These pages are KDP filler. They break the coloring book + story book rhythm and read as low-effort.
- **No symbol-only redundant pages.** One symbol cluster icon at the title or closing is OK. A whole-page symbol mandala is redundant with the rest of the book's iconography and produces a coloring page kids find boring. Drop it.
- **Bookplate, grown-ups note, and closing blessing are the only allowed non-narrative pages.** Bookplate at p2. Grown-ups note as ONE framed page near the back. Closing blessing as the final text page. That's it.
- **Page count is whatever the stories need.** 30, 32, 34, 36 — driven by story content, not template. Krishna shipped at 32pp. Ganesha new at 34pp. Hanuman new at 32pp. Rama new at 32pp. Lakshmi new at 30pp.

### Story canon mining — bring in side-characters

For each title-deity, mine the canon for **character-rich story beats** before writing. Don't fall back on abstract teaching pages. Examples:

- **Ganesha** — origin (Parvati/Shiva/elephant), race around the world (Kartikeya/peacock), broken tusk (Vyasa/Mahabharata), laughing moon (parenting-psych beat), Mooshik adoption
- **Hanuman** — baby + sun, ocean leap, Jambavan reminds (named), Sita + ring scene, sindoor/orange paint story, Sanjeevani mountain, carrying Rama on shoulders
- **Rama and Diwali** — Sita's swayamvara (bow lifting), Shabari + sweet berries (folk), the little squirrel + the bridge (folk), Ravana the scholar (10-headed iconographic coloring page, kid-safe framing), Hanuman the vanara friend
- **Lakshmi and Saraswati** — Saraswati gives Brahma the alphabet, the wise hamsa swan separating milk from water, Lakshmi rising from the churned ocean (softened samudra manthan), Lakshmi visiting the home that shares (folk), the two goddess friends together
- **Krishna** (already shipped) — Yashoda, Maakhan-stealing, Sudama (friend), Arjuna in the field, Gau Mata, Rasleela

If a book is shaping up with the title-deity alone on every page, **stop and mine the canon again.** Find the side-characters.

### Visual character style — ALL deities as cute young children (locked 2026-05-17)

Every deity character across every book in this series is drawn as a CUTE YOUNG CHILD, regardless of the canonical story context. This is the rule that makes the line feel like one shelf.

**Why this rule exists.** Krishna's book (book 1) shipped feeling right because the canonical Krishna story is the Maakhan-Chor / butter-thief kid Krishna — so kid-Krishna is what got drawn. Hanuman's canonical stories (ocean leap, carrying the mountain, bowing to Rama) are mostly "grown-up helper Hanuman" in tradition — so the first round of Hanuman raws came out adult-styled and broke the series tone. Same risk for Rama (warrior-prince adult), Lakshmi/Saraswati (mature goddesses), Ganesha (already kid in canon — safe). Locking this rule keeps the line consistent.

**The rule.**

- Hanuman, Krishna, Ganesha, Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, Lakshmi, Saraswati, and every supporting deity figure is drawn as a **cute young child** throughout the line — chibi-ish proportions, big round head about 1/3 the figure's height, soft round body, big expressive eyes, tiny nose, soft cute smile.
- Visual register: **kids' storybook character**, NOT temple iconography. Think Sanjay's Super Team / soft Pixar-flat / Indian-folk kids cartoon — but rendered as B&W coloring-book line art.
- This applies even when the story-context is mythologically adult (kid-Hanuman carrying a mountain, kid-Rama with a bow at swayamvara, kid-Sita offering a flower, etc.). The COMPOSITION can follow the canonical scene; the FIGURES inside it are always children.
- Animal/supporting figures (Jambavan the bear, Mooshik the mouse, Uluka the owl, the hamsa swan, the squirrel of the Setubandha) are drawn as cute storybook animals — rounded shapes, soft features — paired to match the kid-deity style.
- Antagonists named in text (Ravana, etc.) follow the same rule when ever depicted: drawn as a young child version with the complexity framing intact (clever, scholarly, devotional — never scary).
- Iconography preserved as costume / accessory: crown, topknot, tail (Hanuman), peacock feather (Krishna), modak + mouse (Ganesha), bow (Rama), folded hands. Render them at child-figure scale so they read as accessories, not as warrior-gear.
- **Exception:** the explicit "baby" beats in the story canon (baby Hanuman + sun, baby Krishna + butter) get drawn at the smaller toddler register — round-toddler chibi, not the slightly-older kid-child register. Both are "children"; the baby version is the more rounded extreme.

**How to apply in art briefs.** Every per-page art brief in production-notes.md and IMAGEGEN-PROMPTS.md must specify the figure as "young child" / "kid version" / "cute child Hanuman" etc. Do NOT write "Hanuman" or "Rama" without the kid qualifier — the image model will default to mythologically-mature renderings every time. This rule is brand-locked; do not soften.

**Visual-system anchor for image gen.** Reference the rule explicitly in the IMAGEGEN-PROMPTS.md global style block of every book, with a one-liner like: "All deity figures are cute young children — chibi proportions, big head, round body, soft cute storybook face. Iconography (crown, tail, etc.) preserved as accessories at child scale."

## Adult Line

Adult books may be richer and more contemplative, but still avoid overclaiming. Deities can be presented as ways of touching qualities of the whole, but that is Adrian's practitioner frame, not a universal claim.

Separate adult visionary/psychedelic-aware books from the kids/yoga parent brand.

## Banned Or High-Risk Register

Avoid: manifest, abundance as generic affirmation filler, queen, magic, deserve, embark, delve, tapestry, moreover, furthermore, in essence, in conclusion, let's explore, unlock the power, in today's fast-paced world, you got this, high vibration, your truth.

Exception: culturally accurate terms such as Lakshmi's association with abundance may be used only with context and care.
